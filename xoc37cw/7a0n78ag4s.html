STEP 3Your reasonable salary

The IRS expects you to pay yourself what you would have to pay someone else to do your job before you take distributions. There is no percentage rule. The defensible number comes from market pay for your actual duties and hours.

Your salary is not your whole paycheck

Your salary is your W-2 pay: the part that runs through payroll with taxes taken out. Your true monthly income is your salary plus your distribution. Your payroll provider can add the distribution to the same direct deposit as a separate, untaxed line, so you still get one deposit each payday. It just has to be coded as a distribution, not wages.

        Where your savings come from

        An S corp saves money because the profit you take as distributions skips Social Security and Medicare tax (15.3%). Here is that savings, and what offsets it.

        HOW IT WORKS

        • Salary is your W-2 pay and runs through payroll. Social Security and Medicare (7.65% from you, 7.65% from the business) apply only to salary.
        • Distributions are the rest of the profit you take out. Your payroll provider can add them to your paycheck deposit as an untaxed line, coded as a distribution, not wages. They are still taxed for income tax, but not for Social Security and Medicare. That difference is where S corp savings come from.
        • Taxes through payroll: the planner withholds your full estimated federal and state income tax from your paychecks, including the tax on profit you take as distributions. Payroll figures its regular withholding from your W-4 using the IRS percentage method (Publication 15-T, 2020 or later W-4, Step 2 not checked); the planner shows the extra to add so the total covers your distribution too. Payroll software can differ by a few dollars, and state withholding is an estimate. The IRS treats withholding as paid evenly through the year, so you can usually skip quarterly estimates.
        • IRA or Roth IRA through payroll: your S corp can take IRA money out of each paycheck and send it to your own IRA. It comes out after tax. A traditional IRA may be deductible on your return; if you also have a 401(k), that deduction can phase out at higher incomes, so the planner doesn't assume it. Roth IRAs have income limits.
        • Unemployment tax: as an employee of your S corp, the business pays federal unemployment tax (0.6% of your first $7,000 of wages, more in states that owe the federal fund) and state unemployment tax at your state's rate on its wage base. The planner fills in each state's 2026 new-employer rate; replace it with the rate on your state notice. Alaska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania also take a small employee share.
        • Health insurance: when the S corp pays an owner's health insurance, it is reported on the W-2 for income tax but not Social Security or Medicare, and the owner can usually deduct it on the personal return. The old way assumes you pay the same premiums yourself and deduct them.
        • 401(k): your contribution has a yearly limit, higher at 50 and older. The business can add up to 25% of your salary. The planner applies the limits for the tax year you pick.
        • Uses the federal tax brackets, standard deduction (the amount everyone can subtract before tax), the 20% deduction for business owners (called QBI) and the Social Security wage cap for the tax year you pick.
        • 2027 figures: brackets, standard deduction and the $192,300 Social Security cap are official. The 401(k) limits ($25,500, $8,500 catch-up, $75,000 total) and the business-owner deduction thresholds are projected until the IRS confirms them. State figures are 2026 until states publish 2027.
        • State tax uses your state's 2026 brackets, standard deduction and personal exemption. California figures come from the Franchise Tax Board and EDD. Phase-outs for high earners in some states are not included.
        • Head of household and married filing separately use exact 2026 federal brackets. For state tax they use that state's single-filer brackets, except California head of household, which gets California's $11,800 standard deduction. That makes the state estimate slightly high for head of household.
